SIP Fit

A small feature inside an investing app that helps first-time investors keep their SIP going through tight months: skip or adjust for free, before a debit fails.

Not investment advice. It never recommends funds or predicts markets. It only helps people time their own SIP and use options the app already offers.

Release gate

Before release, the AI has to pass two fixed test sets. The expected answers were written before running, and cases were adapted from real public posts and survey answers, with no personal data.

  • Month Map reading (24 cases): does the AI pull out the right payday, bill days and heavy months from a sentence? Scored by exact match per field. Includes Hinglish, vague dates, variable income, account numbers and an instruction-hijack attempt.
  • Replies and safety (16 cases): does a reply to a check-in map to the right option, and does it refuse fund or market questions? Every final answer must pass the safety guard.

Gate: payday ≥ 90%, heavy months ≥ 85%, bill days ≥ 80%, asks when unclear 100%, replies understood ≥ 80%, final answers safe 100%, fallback needed ≤ 20%, tone judge agrees with humans ≥ 90%.

The problem it solves

   
First-time investors rarely lose their SIP because they decide to quit. They lose it in a month when the SIP and their money don't line up. On Groww, skipping is free but must be requested more than 2 days before the debit. The bank's required notice arrives only about 24 hours before. Groww also sends SIP reminders, but we couldn't find when. So people who are short find out too late, the debit fails, the bank charges a fee, and many give up. In our survey of 13 investors, 8 didn't know whether their app could pause, and 6 hadn't chosen their debit date deliberately.

  

  

   
Three steps

   
    
    
  1. Fit: three quick questions after the first instalment succeeds: how income arrives (fixed date, last working day, it varies, or from savings), months that cost more, and an optional sentence the AI reads. Rules suggest a debit date a few days after payday and after regular bills, and list only the months that need a reminder. You can edit anything, and you pick what you'd rather do in a tight month.
    2. 
    
  2. Bend: before those months, a calm message arrives 5 days before the debit, leaving 2 days before the free-skip deadline, with two buttons: "Keep" or your own plan. "Pay a smaller amount" is the suggested plan, because a smaller payment still counts as an active month and a skip doesn't. Tapping the message opens all the options. Doing nothing changes nothing.
    3. 
    
  3. Recover: works for everyone, even without setup. If a debit fails, you're told your invested money is safe, can pay it by UPI within Groww's 2-working-day window, and can move the date if it doesn't suit your payday. Missing 3 in a row cancels the SIP, and the app says so plainly before it can happen.
    4.

Why it's designed this way

   
    
    
  • Different kinds of first-time investor: salary on a fixed date (weekend paydays are checked), salary on the last working day, income that varies (no monthly messages unless they switch them on, and the date is set after most money usually arrives), and SIPs paid from savings or family money (payday doesn't matter, only heavy months).
    • 
    
  • Only what needs attention: the Month Map lists just the months we'll check in on, instead of labelling all twelve.
    • 
    
  • Familiar parts: a SIP details page with one summary row (details one tap away), a lock-screen notification with at most two buttons where tapping the message opens the full choice, bottom sheets for choices, and a confirmation screen after every action. These are the patterns people already use in investing apps.
    • 
    
  • Cancel shows gentler options first: skipping the next instalment or lowering the amount, with the facts about what cancelling does. Cancelling still takes one tap.
    • 
    
  • Free skips act like an "emergency reserve": research found people with a small built-in reserve keep going after a slip, while people with a hard goal give up (Sharif & Shu, 2019).
    • 
    
  • Confirm or undo: anything that moves money or can't be reversed asks first. Everything easy to reverse happens in one tap with an Undo, because asking "are you sure?" every time teaches people to stop reading (Nielsen: user control and freedom, error prevention).
    • 
    
  • A plan made in advance ("if a month is tight, I'd rather skip it") makes follow-through more likely (Milkman et al., 2011).
    • 
    
  • A message, not a banner: people avoid looking at their investments when news may be bad (the ostrich effect), so help has to come to them.
    • 
    
  • Few messages: only before risky debits, so they don't become noise.
    • 
    
  • Kept small: the default is always "keep", reminders come only before months that might be tight, and "pay less" sits next to "skip", because easy exits can weaken saving.
    •

Limits

   
    
    
  • Weekends are checked; bank holidays aren't, so a holiday payday may not be flagged.
    • 
    
  • It doesn't see bank balances. It only knows what you tell it. A later version could use consented bank data (Account Aggregator).
    • 
    
  • Rules are as of November 2026. In a real launch they would come straight from the platform's own systems.
    • 
    
  • That paying a pending instalment counts as that month's successful instalment is our reading of how it works.
